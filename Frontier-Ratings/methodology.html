What this is, and is not

The purpose of Frontier Ratings is to let technology leaders monitor the evolving vendor landscape for their most significant AI and cybersecurity challenges: how the market leaders change as the risk evolves, and when new solutions enter the marketplace. The ratings are built for that. They are relative to the field being tracked, recalculated at every weekly update, and every movement is recorded under Market watch.

  • Who is leading now. Product capability (capability, maturity, scalability) ranks products and sets the tier. “Top rated” is whoever is within 0.2 of the best in the category this week, so the label moves when the field moves. It is a comparison, so a category with only one rated product has no top-rated product: that product is shown as established or emerging on its own evidence.
  • Who is entering. “Emerging” marks newer solutions and those not yet proven at scale. New products are added as they appear and start in the tier their evidence supports.
  • Whether a bank can adopt it. Bank readiness (visibility, standards, vendor viability, country risk) is shown beside the rank and never changes it.
  • The four-square chart. Pages plot product capability against bank readiness, one dot per product. The dividing lines are the medians of the products shown, so the four squares describe the current field rather than a fixed bar.

Independent informational analysis. Not procurement, legal or investment advice. Scores are opinions derived from the published method. A product does not make a bank compliant with anything.

No vendor pays to be listed, scored or moved. Research and scoring are done by an AI agent following the method on this page, on a weekly schedule, with no human review before publication. Automated validation is the safeguard; its rules are listed below.

Which vendors are covered

This site covers cybersecurity and AI firms that aim at the financial services sector. A firm qualifies when any one of these is true and a public source shows it:

  1. Markets to financial services. It has a dedicated financial services or banking page, or it markets banking-specific solutions.
  2. Names financial institution customers. It names bank or financial institution customers on the public record.
  3. Takes part in a financial-services event or body. It appears as a sponsor, exhibitor or supporting member at a financial-services-focused event or body, such as an FS-ISAC summit or a banking security or technology conference.
  • The signal is recorded. Each vendor’s page says which signal or signals qualified it, with the source and the date it was read.
  • Qualifying is not scoring. The signals decide only whether a firm is covered. They are not read by the scoring code and cannot move a score. A new vendor goes through the same full review as every other and starts in whatever tier its evidence supports.
  • How new firms are found. Every weekly update reads the published sponsor, exhibitor and member lists of financial-services events and bodies, and the firms those lead to. Many of these lists are PDFs, image logo walls or archived pages. A list that cannot be read is logged as “could not be checked”. Membership is never inferred.
  • How many are added. While coverage is being built out there is no weekly cap: every qualifying firm found is given a full review in the update that finds it, as far as that update can reach. Each addition is still a full review from live sources and must pass every validation rule. Firms not reached wait in a queue and are taken oldest first.
  • A category for every firm. If a qualifying firm fits no existing category, a new one is created with its own capability list, and the change is listed in the rubric history.
  • The log. What each update found is in the run history: candidates found, added, rejected with the reason, and any that could not be verified.
  • About banks. Where a source names a bank as a customer, this site records only what that source says. It does not describe or imply any bank’s internal security.

The rule was first published with the 23 Nov 2026 dataset. The vendors tracked before that date are the original seed set, chosen by category before the rule existed; their qualifying signals have not yet been recorded and their pages say so.

Sales, support and update links

Every vendor and product page opens with four links a buyer needs first: who to contact for sales, where to get support, where release notes and product updates are published, and where security advisories are published.

  • Where each one lives. Company-wide links (sales, the general support portal) are held on the vendor. Links for one product (its release notes, changelog or support page) are held on the product. Where a vendor publishes one only at company level, the product page shows the company-wide link and says so.
  • Each link is a sourced fact. It is recorded only after the page was opened, with the date it was read, and it goes through the same weekly link check as every other source. A link found dead is replaced or withdrawn before the update is published.
  • Three states, never a blank. A confirmed link; “no public evidence”, where a researcher looked and found none; or “not yet checked”, where only the automated look has run. The automated look reads the vendor’s own pages and never records “no public evidence” by itself: a script not finding a page is not evidence that it does not exist.
  • The backlog. Up to 40 records with links still to check are taken by a researcher in each update, so the first full pass is spread over several weeks. The run history shows how many remain.
  • Not scored. Quick links never enter a score.

Source policy

  • Free public sources only. No paid data.
  • Every fact is researched live and tied to a source with its address, type, publisher and retrieval date. Nothing is written from an AI model’s memory.
  • Every claim and score is labelled Vendor-claimed or Independently evidenced. Independent means a source other than the vendor supports it: a filing, a regulator or certification registry, independent press, or an independent test.
  • Analyst-firm reports are cited only through what a vendor or the press publicly quotes.
  • Source text is not copied. Summaries are our own; at most one attributed quote of 200 characters per event.
  • Two sources count as independent of each other only when their publishers are different organisations and neither is the vendor.

Evidence classes

Every claim and score carries one of three labels. For a score, the label is that of the evidence its highest anchor depends on.

  • Vendor-claimed The vendor says so in marketing pages, press releases, blog posts or other general statements. Scores capped at 3.
  • Vendor-documented The vendor's own material, but specific and checkable: technical documentation, API references, release notes, published service terms and lifecycle policies, trust-centre documents, and statements in audited securities filings. Scores capped at 4.
  • Independently evidenced A source other than the vendor supports it: a regulator or certification registry, an independent test, independent press, or a customer's own publication. No cap.

Confidence

  • high: Primary or regulator source, or two independent sources that agree, retrieved within the last 12 months.
  • medium: One independent source, or a specific and checkable vendor statement (for example a published sub-processor list).
  • low: A single general vendor statement, a source older than 12 months, or sources that partly conflict.

No public evidence

If a fact cannot be sourced, the field is recorded as No public evidence and linked to a knowledge gap: what was searched, why it matters, and the question a bank should put to the vendor. “No public evidence” is never shown as zero, “no” or blank, and never counts against a score. This dataset has 4868 open gaps.

Scoring rubric (version 0.4.2)

Each product is placed on a whole rubric level, 0 to 5, on seven dimensions. Higher is always better; on country risk 5 means the lowest risk. The level is the highest anchor whose description is fully met by cited evidence.

From rubric level to a one-decimal score

Many products sit on the same rubric level, so the score shown is the level plus a small, published adjustment for how well that level is evidenced. It moves a score by a few tenths at most and never outside 0 to 5. Every score on the site can be selected to see its level, each adjustment, the reasoning and the sources.

  • Who the evidence comes from: a source other than the vendor, +0.2; the vendor’s own documentation or filings, 0; the vendor’s general claims only, -0.2.
  • Confidence: high, +0.1; medium, 0; low, -0.1.
  • Number of sources: three or more, +0.1; two, 0; one, -0.1.
  • Capability only, coverage of the category’s capability list: the share of listed capabilities the product supports (a partly supported one counts as half), compared with 80%, to the nearest tenth and limited to 0.2 either way. Full coverage adds 0.2.

The adjustment reflects the strength of the evidence, not a finer judgement of the product. A difference of one or two tenths between two products is small; a full rubric level is the meaningful step.

Capability and differentiation: How completely does the product do the job its category defines, and what does it do that alternatives do not?

Evidence considered: Coverage of the category's capability list; Public documentation; Independent tests and evaluations; Customer evidence.

0Does not deliver the category's core function.
1Delivers part of the core function; most listed capabilities are absent.
2Delivers the core function; several listed capabilities are partial or absent.
3Covers most listed capabilities.
4Covers all or nearly all listed capabilities, shown in published technical documentation or equally specific vendor material.
5The above, with capability confirmed by a source other than the vendor: an independent test, evaluation or validation of the product, or a deployment published by a named bank or comparable regulated customer.
Maturity: How proven is the product in production?

Evidence considered: Time generally available; Release history; Named production customers; Documentation depth; Stability of the roadmap.

0Announced only; nothing can be used.
1Private preview or beta; no production customers evidenced.
2Generally available for under a year, or few production customers evidenced.
3Generally available for one to three years with named production customers and a regular release history.
4Generally available for more than three years, with adoption at scale evidenced: named regulated-industry customers, or customer numbers disclosed in an audited filing or equivalent.
5The above, with bank or comparable regulated customers in production confirmed by a source other than the vendor, and a published support and deprecation policy.
Enterprise scalability: Can a large bank run it across the enterprise?

Evidence considered: Published limits and SLAs; Deployment models; Identity and access integration; Multi-region and tenancy options; Evidence of large deployments; Counts of large customers in audited filings.

0No evidence it runs beyond a single team or pilot.
1Small deployments only; no single sign-on or role-based access.
2One of these is evidenced: enterprise identity integration, a published availability commitment, documented scale limits, multi-region or dedicated deployment options, or disclosed large-enterprise customers.
3At least two of those five are evidenced.
4Adoption at large-enterprise scale is shown in documentation, an audited filing or independent press, together with multi-region or dedicated deployment options or a published availability commitment.
5The above, plus deployment at a bank or comparable regulated enterprise confirmed by a source other than the vendor, or independently published performance at that scale.
Observability: What visibility does the product give the bank into what it is doing?

Evidence considered: Logging; Audit trails; Explainability of decisions or outputs; Telemetry export; SIEM integration; Evidence it can produce for auditors and regulators.

0No logs or audit trail available to the customer.
1Basic activity logs viewable in the product only; no export.
2Logs can be exported manually; audit trail is partial; no SIEM integration.
3Documented audit trail, API or streaming log export, and at least one supported SIEM integration.
4The above, plus decision or output explanations where relevant, configurable retention, and tamper-evident audit records.
5The above, plus documented evidence packs or reports designed for auditors and regulators, independently confirmed in use.
Standards alignment: Which recognised standards has the vendor been assessed against, and does the product map to the frameworks banks use?

Evidence considered: Current certificates and attestation reports; Scope of each attestation; Published control mappings; Registry entries.

0No attestations or mappings.
1Self-declared alignment only.
2One current third-party attestation; scope unclear or excludes the product.
3Two or more current third-party attestations whose scope covers the product.
4The above, plus published control mappings to the frameworks relevant to the category.
5The above, verifiable in a public registry or by a published report, including the standards specific to the category.
Vendor viability: How likely is the vendor to be supporting this product in three years?

Evidence considered: Funding and investors; Public financials; Runway signals; Layoffs; Ownership changes.

0Closed, or closure announced.
1Credible reports of distress: failed funding, insolvency proceedings, or repeated deep layoffs.
2Early-stage or thinly funded, or recent layoffs with no offsetting evidence.
3Funded within the last two years or revenue-backed; no distress signals.
4Well funded or profitable, with audited or publicly reported financials.
5Public or equivalently transparent, sustained profitability or very large reserves, and the product is core to the business. Not met where a filing shows total borrowings greater than the latest annual revenue, or a claim or judgment against the vendor greater than its cash and marketable securities.
Country risk (5 = lowest risk): What exposure arises from where the vendor is based, who owns it, and where data and support sit?

Evidence considered: Headquarters; Ownership and major investors; Data-processing and support locations; Sanctions and export-control exposure.

0Vendor or controlling owner is subject to sanctions or export restrictions relevant to the covered jurisdictions.
1Controlling ownership or essential processing in a jurisdiction under restrictive measures from a covered jurisdiction.
2Ownership is opaque, or material processing or support locations are undisclosed.
3Ownership disclosed; processing and support locations disclosed but not selectable by the customer.
4Ownership disclosed; customer can choose processing region among the covered jurisdictions.
5The above, plus support access can be restricted to chosen jurisdictions and no sanctions or export-control exposure was found.

Scores, pillars and tiers

  • Composite = sum of (weight × score) ÷ sum of weights, over scored dimensions only.
  • Coverage = share of the axis weight that is scored. Shown as “% evidenced”. Below 60%, a product is not ranked; it is listed as “not enough public evidence”.
  • Composite confidence = the lowest confidence among scored dimensions carrying at least 10% of the axis weight.
  • Evidence caps: a score supported only by vendor claims cannot exceed 3. A score supported by the vendor’s own specific, checkable material (technical documentation, published terms, audited filings) cannot exceed 4. A 5 needs a source other than the vendor.

Pillars

Each of the seven dimensions is shown as a column so that products can be compared at a glance: three for product capability, then four for bank readiness.

  • Capability: How completely it addresses the problem today. Capability and differentiation
  • Maturity: How proven it is in production, which separates leaders from new entrants. Maturity
  • Scalability: Whether a large bank can run it at enterprise scale. Enterprise scalability
  • Visibility: What the bank can see and evidence. Observability
  • Standards: Certifications and standards the product is attested against. Standards alignment
  • Viability: How likely the vendor is to last. Vendor viability
  • Country risk: Where the vendor is based and owned (higher is safer). Country risk (5 = lowest risk)

Tiers

Tiers are relative to the products tracked in the same category, so each category shows its spectrum from top rated to emerging products. “Top rated” means within 0.2 points of the category’s best score. It describes the score on public evidence, not market share or sales, so a smaller vendor with well-documented products can be top rated above a much larger one. A tier is not an endorsement.

  • Top rated: Leading its category now: product capability within 0.2 of the best, on enough public evidence to judge. Leaders change as products and risks evolve. This is about the score, not market share.
  • Established: Proven in production, but not among the category's leaders this week. Watch for movement in either direction.
  • Emerging: A newer solution entering the market, or one whose production use at scale is not yet evidenced. This is where the next leaders come from.
  • Not enough public evidence: Less than 60% of the score could be established from public sources. That says nothing about the product itself.

Market presence

Market presence is a separate label for how big and established the company behind a product is. It is shown beside the score and never mixed into it, so the two can disagree: where a smaller vendor is top rated above a major one, both facts are visible. It is not market share, which is not available from free public sources.

It is worked out from facts already on the vendor’s record, by fixed thresholds:

  • Size: 5,000 or more employees, 3 points; 1,000 or more employees, 2 points; 250 or more employees, 1 points. Where annual revenue has been researched: US$1 billion or more, 3; US$250 million or more, 2; US$50 million or more, 1. The higher of the two counts.
  • Capital: listed on a stock exchange, 2 points; or capital raised of US$5 billion or more, 5; US$1 billion or more, 3; US$250 million or more, 1. The latest funding round is used when no total is recorded. Euro and sterling amounts are compared at face value.
  • Years in business: 15 or more, 2 points; 7 or more, 1 points.
  • Analyst coverage: 1 point when the vendor or the press publicly quotes an analyst report naming it.
  • A vendor owned by another tracked company takes its owner’s level when that is higher.
  • Major vendor (5 points or more): Large and long-established on the public record: size, capital, years in business and analyst coverage. 81 vendors.
  • Mid-size vendor (3 to 4 points): Substantial on the public record, below the largest vendors. 49 vendors.
  • Smaller vendor (fewer than 3 points): Smaller or newer on the public record: modest headcount or capital, or recently founded. 119 vendors.
  • Size not public (no fact about size or capital on record): Neither headcount, stock listing nor capital raised could be established from public sources. 31 vendors.

Limits: headcount is recorded for about half the vendors, and privately held companies disclose little, so “size not public” is common and “smaller vendor” can understate a company whose figures were not found. Revenue, customer numbers and analyst placements have a place in the record but have not yet been researched for most vendors.

Scoping Wizard formula

The wizard makes no AI calls. The same inputs and dataset version always return the same result.

  1. Filter. Take products in the chosen category. Exclude, showing the reason and its source: a vendor recorded as closed; a vendor recorded as distressed (at low risk appetite only); a product that is end of sale, end of life or absorbed; a product that does not offer a required deployment model; one whose customer data trains models when “no training” is required; one without customer-managed keys when those are required. If there is no public evidence on a constraint, the product is not excluded: it is flagged and the question goes to the top of its due-diligence list.
  2. Weight. Start from both default axes at half weight each. Multiply each priority group by the visitor’s setting (0 to 3): Product capability; Observability; Standards alignment; Vendor viability; Country risk. Apply bank size: community: Enterprise scalability ×0.75; regional: no change; large: Enterprise scalability ×1.5, Maturity ×1.5. Apply risk appetite to country risk and vendor viability: low ×1.5, medium ×1, high ×0.75. Rescale to sum to 1.
  3. Score. Fit = composite with those weights, shown out of 100. Products with under 50% coverage are listed separately as not ranked.
  4. Rank. By fit; ties by coverage, then by number of chosen jurisdictions with at least one sourced regulatory mapping, then by product id.
  5. Explain. Each result shows every dimension’s weight and score, its flags, and due-diligence questions taken from its open knowledge gaps.

Jurisdictions affect tie-breaks and what is displayed; they do not change the fit figure, because a sourced mapping is evidence of vendor documentation, not of suitability.

Regulatory and standards mapping

The site shows standards, frameworks and attestation schemes. Regulations and supervisory guidance are no longer shown. Each standard has a registry record with its issuer, version, status and effective date, verified from the issuing body’s own site and re-verified at least every 90 days. Mappings are worded “Supports the bank in …”. A product does not make a bank compliant, and no mapping here says so. A mapping exists only where a source supports it, so an empty cell means “nothing sourced”, not “not relevant”.

Adverse claims

Layoffs, shutdowns, distress, security incidents, outages, regulatory actions, litigation, sanctions and unintended consequences are adverse claims. Each needs one primary filing or regulator source, the vendor’s own disclosure, or two independent press sources from different publishers. Wording is neutral and attributed (“Reported by X on date”). A claim that does not meet this bar is not published.

What counts as an attestation

  • An attestation counts toward a product’s standards score only if its stated scope covers that product. A government or regulated edition of the same product counts, and the profile says so.
  • Where the scope is unpublished or available only on request, it is treated as unclear, however many certifications the vendor lists.
  • Self-assessments, in-process or legacy statuses and expired reports are not counted.
  • Where a public registry exists (FedRAMP Marketplace, NIST’s validated-modules list, CSA STAR), the profile records what the registry shows, including where it differs from the vendor’s own statement.

How lawsuits are weighed

  • Routine matters count as minor: individual employment claims, patent-assertion suits, disputes over a name or logo, and suits the vendor brought itself.
  • A matter counts as material only when a source describes what it is about: a company filing, a court document, a regulator’s notice or press coverage. A court docket listing on its own is recorded but does not move a score.
  • Two outlets relaying the same original report count as one source.
  • Court searches cover a US federal docket index. State courts and courts outside the US are not covered, so “no docket found” is not “no litigation”.

Dataset versions and permanent links

Each weekly run cuts a dataset version named by its date. Records are stored once by content hash and a version is a short list of which records it contains, so unchanged records add nothing and any past version can be rebuilt exactly. Versions published so far: 2026-11-20, 2026-11-22, 2026-11-23.

  • /v/DATE/products/… and /v/DATE/vendors/… never change. They show their date and a notice when a newer version exists.
  • /products/… and /vendors/… show the latest.
  • Comparison, leaderboard, regulations, report and Scoping Wizard links carry their inputs and, when made permanent, the dataset version, so they reproduce exactly.
  • Vendors that are acquired or closed keep their pages and history. Events are never edited; a correction is a new event that supersedes the old one.

Known limitations

  • Public sources favour large, listed and well-documented vendors. Smaller vendors show more fields with no public evidence, and therefore lower coverage, not lower scores.
  • Vendor claims are often the only source for product features. They are labelled and capped.
  • Validation checks that sources exist, links resolve and rules are met. It cannot check that a source was read correctly.
  • Many sites refuse automated link checks; those links are recorded as “could not be checked”, not as working.
  • A link that resolves can still have changed since it was retrieved.
  • Scores are ordinal judgements against anchors. A one-point difference is meaningful; a composite to one decimal place is not a precise measurement.
  • “None found” records a documented search, not proof of absence.
  • The research agent is an AI model made by Anthropic, which is itself one of the vendors rated here. Its record is scored against the same anchors and evidence rules as every other vendor; read it with that in mind.
  • Coverage follows the published inclusion rule and grows as the weekly discovery step reaches more firms, so it is not the whole market: a firm that qualifies may not have been reached yet. Counts on category pages describe tracked vendors only.

Control mapping

Standards and controls links each tracked product capability to the controls of 5 standards whose control lists are published freely and were read from the publisher:

  • NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0: 22 categories, verified 2026-11-21. The 22 categories in Table 1 of NIST CSWP 29, Appendix A. Mapped at category level.
  • NIST SP 800-53 Security and Privacy Controls Rev. 5: 20 control families, verified 2026-11-21. The 20 control families. Family names were read from NIST; the two-letter codes were read from csf.tools, which reproduces the NIST catalogue. Mapped at family level, not to individual controls.
  • NIST Risk Management Framework (SP 800-37) Rev. 2: 7 steps, verified 2026-11-22. The seven steps of the framework. It is a process an organisation follows, not a list of product controls, so a capability is placed in the step it helps a bank carry out. The placement is derived by a fixed rule from the NIST CSF and SP 800-53 mappings (see RMF_RULE), not judged capability by capability.
  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0: 19 categories, verified 2026-11-21. The 19 categories of the AI RMF core. Identifiers are NIST's; the descriptions are short summaries in our own words. NIST states that AI RMF 1.0 is being revised.
  • OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025: 10 risks, verified 2026-11-21. The ten risks of the 2025 edition. These are risks, not controls: a capability is listed where it helps a bank reduce that risk.

A capability is linked to a control when it is the kind of tooling a bank would use to help implement that control. This is the site’s own judgement. It is not an official crosswalk, it is made at the level of categories and control families rather than individual controls, and it never means a product, or a bank using it, satisfies the control. A product appears against a control only where a source shows it has the capability. Of 324 capabilities, 183 are linked to at least one control; the rest are product features with no direct link.

Not mapped: PCI DSS (the PCI Security Standards Council serves the standard and its requirement list only through a gated download, so the requirements could not be verified from the publisher); ISO/IEC 27001, 27701, 42001 and ISO 22301 (iSO control text is sold, not published freely, so the controls could not be verified from the publisher); SOC 2, CSA STAR and FedRAMP (these are attestation schemes. The site records which vendors hold them, with scope, rather than mapping capabilities to their criteria); NIST FIPS 140-3, 203, 204 and 205 (these specify cryptographic modules and algorithms, not a set of controls. Validation and algorithm support are recorded as product capabilities); MITRE ATT&CK and ATLAS (these catalogue attacker techniques, not controls); CRI Profile v2.2 and the CRI Financial Services AI Risk Management Framework (the Cyber Risk Institute offers both as free downloads behind a registration form, so their functions, categories and control objectives could not be read from the publisher. Computed views

Several views are worked out in the browser from the published records. None of them is written by hand, and none changes a published score.

  • Your weights. On the leaderboard a visitor can set their own importance for each dimension. The seven sliders are kept in the page address. Product capability and bank readiness are still weighted separately and never blended; an axis with every slider at zero keeps the published weights.
  • Why this rank. For two neighbouring products, the dimension named is the one whose weighted difference contributes most to the gap, and the one named second pulls most the other way.
  • Must have. A requirement keeps a product only when its record shows full support for the capability, or an evidenced capability that this site relates to the control. It cannot see what a vendor has not published.
  • Evidence age. Measured from a source’s own publication date to the dataset date. A product is marked “mostly older evidence” when it has at least four dated sources and more than half are over 12 months old. Undated sources are counted separately and never aged.
  • White space. A capability that one product or none in its category evidences in full. Categories with a single product are left out.
  • Concentration. For a chosen set of products: the owning companies, their headquarters, and the cloud and AI model providers named on each product’s record, with spellings of the same provider merged.
  • Shortlist. Kept in the visitor’s browser only. Every page it opens carries the chosen products in its own address.

Glossary

Product capability
How good the product is today: what it does, how proven it is in production and whether it runs at enterprise scale. The only score that sets a rank or tier.
Bank readiness
How ready the product and its vendor are for a bank to adopt: visibility, standards, vendor viability and country risk. Shown beside the rank and never changes it.
Tier
Where a product stands within its own category: top rated, established, emerging, or not enough public evidence. It describes the score, not market share.
Top rated
Product capability within 0.2 of the best in the category, on enough public evidence to judge.
Emerging
A newer product, or one whose production use at scale is not yet evidenced on the public record.
Evidence class
Who the evidence comes from. Vendor-claimed is the vendor's general statement. Vendor-documented is the vendor's own specific, checkable material. Independently evidenced comes from someone else.
Vendor-claimed
The only evidence is the vendor's own general statement. A score on this evidence cannot go above 3.
Vendor-documented
The vendor's own material, but specific and checkable: technical documentation, published terms or an audited filing. A score on this evidence cannot go above 4.
Independently evidenced
Supported by a source other than the vendor, such as a regulator, a filing, a test laboratory or independent press.
Confidence
How sure the evaluator is that the evidence supports the level given: high, medium or low. It moves the score by a tenth either way.
No public evidence
It could not be established from public sources. It is left out of the score and never counted as zero. Each one becomes a question to put to the vendor.
Share evidenced
How much of a score's weight could be established from public sources. Below 60%, a product is not ranked.
Rubric level
The whole number from 0 to 5 that the evaluator assigns against the published rubric, before the small adjustment for how it is evidenced.
Market presence
How big and established the company is on the public record: headcount, listing, capital raised, years in business and analyst coverage. It is separate from every score.
Control mapping
This site's own judgement of which standard controls a capability helps a bank implement. It is not an official crosswalk and never means a control is satisfied.
Attestation
A certificate or audit report issued by a third party, such as SOC 2 or ISO 27001. It counts for a product only when it names that product.
Known exploited vulnerability
A flaw the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency has confirmed was used in real attacks. Counts cover everything a vendor sells and are not part of any score.
Dataset version
A dated, unchangeable copy of all the data. A permanent link fixes a page to one version, so it always shows the same result.
White space
A capability or control that no tracked product, or only one, evidences in full. It can be a real market gap or a gap in what vendors publish.
Shortlist
Products you have set aside while browsing. It is kept in this browser only and feeds the compare, portfolio and vendor question pages.
Your weights
Your own importance for each of the seven dimensions. It changes the order on your screen only; the published scores and tiers stay as they are.
